# 2A.1 Our assumptions regarding the mandate and repossession

The mandate is our backstop: it orders a cut only where the voluntary instruments fall short, and repossession lets the state acquire rights, at market value and in instalments, where the market will not supply them. Of the figures here, the one that matters most is how much ordered water actually reaches the lake. Moving it across its plausible range shifts the 2055 lake by 0.72 ft, more than any other lever.

## The mandate floor

**What we assume.** A floor of 1,200 KAF a year of reduced depletion, the top of the 0 to 1,200 KAF range we allow. **Basis.** This is a policy choice, not a measurement. The state's updated water budget puts the inflow needed to reach 4,198 ft by 2055 at about 2,465 KAF a year, against a 2000–2025 average of 1,665 KAF: a gap of 800 KAF a year ([Great Salt Lake Strike Team, 2026](https://d36oiwf74r1rap.cloudfront.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/GSL-Summary-Mar2026.pdf)). Because not all water freed upstream reaches the lake, the cut must be larger than the gap: a 1,100 KAF cut delivers 800 KAF only if about 73% of it arrives. A 1,200 KAF floor is the full gap grossed up for those losses. Because the mandate orders only what the voluntary levers have not already found, the floor is a ceiling on coercion, not a target: in 2055 the mandate orders just 153 KAF. **Verdict:** a policy choice with published support for its size.

### Phase-in and penalty

We phase the mandate in over one year, the fastest setting, and set the penalty for non-compliance at zero. Published targets bound the phase-in between about 8 years (the governor's pledge to refill the lake by 2034, the year of the Winter Olympics) and 29 years (the 2055 planning horizon) ([New York Review of Books, 2026](https://www.nybooks.com/online/2026/06/14/putting-the-lake-to-work-great-salt-lake/)); the Lower Colorado Basin's cut was phased over 3 years ([Greenberg Traurig, 2023](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2023/5/lower-colorado-basin-states-compromise-on-water-conservation)). A one-year phase is at the aggressive end of that range; it is the setting the goal search chose. The penalty can run up to $2,440 an acre-foot, the lake's value. Utah law already lets the State Engineer fine a knowing violation up to $5,000, or $1,000 if not knowing, and order replacement of up to 200% of the water taken ([Utah Legislature, 2026b](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title73/Chapter2/C73-2-S26_1800010118000101.html)). At current lease prices that replacement rule is worth about $500 to $760 an acre-foot. We use no penalty, so the mandate orders the cut and existing enforcement law applies.

## How a mandate widens the market

**What we assume.** A credible mandate widens the voluntary market by 70%: farmers who would not have leased water come forward when a cut may be ordered anyway. **The analogue.** The Lower Colorado River Basin is the closest case. In 2023 the Lower Basin states agreed to conserve an additional 3 million acre-feet through 2026 "to avoid forced cuts", after the federal government asked for reductions of 2 to 4 million acre-feet in 2023 alone ([Hager, 2023](https://www.kuer.org/health-science-environment/2023-05-22/lower-basin-states-offer-landmark-deal-to-cut-back-on-colorado-river-water-for-now)). By 2026, Lower Basin states were conserving an estimated 1.3 MAF a year, of which 770,000 acre-feet were additional, federally compensated reductions on top of 533,000 acre-feet already required, an extra share of 1.44 ([Congressional Research Service, 2026](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IN11982.html)). We rate both cases 3 out of 5: the mechanism is right, but the threat was bundled with large federal payments, and the Secretary of the Interior holds powers over Lower Basin water that Utah's State Engineer does not hold over the lake's tributaries. In the Upper Basin, a 1.7-fold rise in participation between 2023 and 2024, from 64 to 109 projects, matches our 0.7 exactly, but was caused by a take-it-or-leave-it fixed price, not a threat ([Smith, 2024](https://insideclimatenews.org/news/02062024/colorado-river-upper-basin-conservation-program/)); we rate it 2. We keep 0.7, below the Lower Basin figure, because Utah's backstop is a state mandate without federal money behind it.

## Repossession

**What we assume.** The state may compulsorily acquire up to 140 KAF a year of water rights, paying in instalments at 3% interest. **Basis.** A policy choice and a legal limit: compulsory acquisition at market value takes time to process. No published figure says how much water Utah could acquire this way in a year. For scale, 10% of agricultural depletion is about 158 KAF a year, and the state's own near-term goal is to secure 100,000 acre-feet in total, temporary or permanent ([Office of the Great Salt Lake Commissioner, 2024](https://greatsaltlake.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Great-Salt-Lake-Strategic-Plan.pdf)). Our cap of 140 KAF a year is near the top of the 0 to 150 KAF range. **Verdict:** no evidence; an assumption at the edge of its range.

**Interest.** The 3% rate sits between the 1.82% the state pays on average on its outstanding general obligation debt ([Utah State Treasurer, 2025a](https://treasurer.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025-Debt-Affordability-Study_Released-November-1.pdf)) and the roughly 4% its investment pool was earning at the end of 2025 ([Utah State Treasurer, 2025b](https://treasurer.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/20251231-PTIF-Stats-and-Holdings.pdf)). **Verdict:** corrected from 1.5%, and labelled an assumption.

**Which ground is taken.** We assume repossessed ground is 26% less productive than average, because the selection is weighted away from the best land. The state's yield classes support a discount of about 23% or more for the lowest third of acres ([Utah Division of Water Resources, 2026a](https://water.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/GSL-BIP_AgWaterOptimizationReport_FINAL_2026-04-21.pdf)). Compulsory acquisition still pays fair market value; the discount comes from which land is chosen, not from underpaying for it.

## Delivering ordered water

**What we assume.** Water freed by an order arrives at the lake at 71% of the rate of water the state holds as a right. **Basis.** A cut ordered on a farm is harder to track than a right the state holds: the freed water stays in the river with no owner protecting it, and a downstream diverter may take it. The state's own reports make the point that conservation "may not benefit the lake if the water is used and depleted by downstream users" ([Great Salt Lake Strike Team, 2025](https://d36oiwf74r1rap.cloudfront.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/GSL-Jan2025.pdf)). **The analogue.** In the Upper Colorado's conservation programme, water left in streams "is not tracked to Lake Powell", and officials "are not measuring how much of that conserved water ends up" there ([Smith, 2024](https://insideclimatenews.org/news/02062024/colorado-river-upper-basin-conservation-program/)). We rate this 2: it shows that the delivered share of unprotected water is unmeasured, not what it is. **Verdict:** no evidence. No published study compares how much mandated and acquired water reaches a lake. This is one of the most consequential unmeasured numbers in our model, and the strongest argument for buying water rather than ordering it.
