# 1.6 Alfalfa

Alfalfa and other hay consume 1,025 KAF of the basin's water a year, 43% of all human use, and earn $368 for each acre-foot. Our proposal takes most of its water from hay because that is where the volume is and where the value is lowest. It does so by paying farmers and leaving the choice of whether to sell with them. We are not hostile to farming: an acre-foot is worth more than six times as much in the lake as in hay, and our package pays for that difference.

## How much water alfalfa uses, and why

Alfalfa is a perennial that grows from early spring to autumn and is cut several times a season, so it transpires water for far longer than a grain crop. Near Salt Lake City an acre of alfalfa uses 37.22 inches of water a season. Rain supplies part of that, and the rest, 28.97 inches or 2.41 ft, has to come from irrigation ([Hill et al., 2011](https://extension.usu.edu/irrigation/files/Appendix_I_ET_NWS_Stations.pdf)). Farmers apply more than the crop consumes, because some water runs off or seeps back to the river. Utah alfalfa growers apply an average of 2.4 acre-feet an acre: 2.6 on sprinkler and pivot fields, 2.0 on flood-irrigated ones ([USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service, 2024b](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Publications/AgCensus/2022/Online_Resources/Farm_and_Ranch_Irrigation_Survey/iwms.pdf)).

Across the basin, the hay and turf group accounts for 64.7% of agricultural depletion, and nearly all of it is alfalfa and grass hay ([Utah Division of Water Resources, 2026a](https://water.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/GSL-BIP_AgWaterOptimizationReport_FINAL_2026-04-21.pdf)). The 2022 Census of Agriculture counts about 228,000 irrigated acres of hay in the twelve Utah counties of the basin ([USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service, 2024c](https://nass.usda.gov/Publications/AgCensus/2022/Full_Report/Volume_1,_Chapter_2_County_Level/Utah/st49_2_024_024.pdf)).

## What it earns

Utah harvested 490,000 acres of alfalfa in 2023 and produced 1.96 million tons, 12th among the states. All hay covered 660,000 acres ([USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service, 2024a](https://data.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/Utah/Publications/Annual_Statistical_Bulletin/2024-Agricultural-Statistics.pdf)). Over 2019–2023 the alfalfa crop was worth $889 an acre, and divided by the 2.41 ft of irrigation water it consumes, that is $368 an acre-foot.

The price swings. Utah alfalfa sold for $127 a ton in 2016, $297 in 2022 and $222 in 2023 ([USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service, 2024a](https://data.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/Utah/Publications/Annual_Statistical_Bulletin/2024-Agricultural-Statistics.pdf)). At 2025 prices, the value per acre-foot would be about $310 rather than $368.

What the farmer keeps is less than the value of the crop. The state's agricultural optimisation report puts the return a grower gives up by not irrigating an acre of alfalfa for a full season at $305. Including $50 an acre for the costs of leasing, it puts the payment growers would accept at $355 an acre, or $236 for each acre-foot of depletion saved ([Utah Division of Water Resources, 2026a](https://water.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/GSL-BIP_AgWaterOptimizationReport_FINAL_2026-04-21.pdf)). Those are the prices our leases and levy have to beat.

## Where the hay goes

Utah exports between 10% and 20% of its hay production ([Utah State University Extension, 2020](https://farmanalysis.usu.edu/static/hay_pricing/pdfs/factsheet.pdf)); growers have put the share going overseas at 6% to 8% ([Salt Lake Tribune, 2023a](https://sltrib.com/news/environment/2023/03/16/should-utah-be-exporting-its)). We assume the basin grows 596,000 tons of forage a year more than its own herds eat. That is above the 150,000 to 520,000 tons those export shares imply for Utah's roughly 2.5 million tons of hay, and no source measures it directly (tab 2B.5).

This matters because it decides who bears the cut. The first tons the proposal takes out are tons that would have left the basin anyway. Only when the cut exceeds the surplus does it reach the feed local dairies and beef herds depend on. In our run it does: the model forgoes 1.78 million tons of hay, three times the surplus, so it also counts $141.9M a year of livestock receipts at risk and 1,265 dairy and beef jobs lost by 2055.

That 1.78 million tons is too high to be all alfalfa. The model counts every acre-foot taken from agriculture as hay at 2.4 acre-feet an acre, so it removes 414,635 acres from irrigation. The basin has only about 228,000 acres of irrigated hay, producing roughly 0.91 million tons. Some of the water the model takes must come from other crops and pasture. We report the model's figures as a ceiling and list the discrepancy in tab 5.

## Utah's place in the national market

The United States grew 49.9 million tons of alfalfa in 2023 and 118.8 million tons of all hay ([USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service, 2024a](https://data.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/Utah/Publications/Annual_Statistical_Bulletin/2024-Agricultural-Statistics.pdf)). Utah's 1.96 million tons is 3.9% of the national alfalfa crop, and the basin's share is about 1.8%. The hay forgone in our run equals 3.6% of the US alfalfa crop and 91% of Utah's. ## What it means for farmers

The losses are real and they fall on farm counties. In 2055 our package costs 7,961 field jobs and 1,265 dairy and beef jobs. Uncompensated farm revenue lost is $210.3M a year in 2035, while the mandate still carries much of the load, and falls to $56.2M by 2055 as bought water replaces ordered water.

The package is built to pay rather than take. Most of the water the lake receives by 2055, 694 KAF a year, comes from rights the state leases or buys. Growers who pay the crop levy pay $41.6M a year, and $37.5M of it comes straight back to them through crop-switching payments. The crop-switching programme spends $40.0M a year to free 100 KAF, at $398 for each acre-foot delivered. A grower who switches keeps farming, keeps the land and is paid for the water the switch frees.

## Alternative crops

### Winter wheat

Winter wheat is the most practical replacement. It grows through the cool months and is harvested in early summer, so it misses the hottest, thirstiest part of the season. Switching an acre from alfalfa to winter wheat saves 0.61 acre-feet of depletion, at a cost of $276 for each acre-foot saved. Spring wheat saves 0.50 acre-feet at $493, and corn 0.53 acre-feet at $424 ([Utah Division of Water Resources, 2026a](https://water.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/GSL-BIP_AgWaterOptimizationReport_FINAL_2026-04-21.pdf)). The same report finds that switching from alfalfa to winter small grains could cut basin agricultural depletion by up to 10%.

The wheat market can absorb it. Utah grew 4.6 million bushels of winter wheat in 2023, against 1,247.7 million bushels nationally ([USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service, 2024a](https://data.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/Utah/Publications/Annual_Statistical_Bulletin/2024-Agricultural-Statistics.pdf)): less than half of one per cent. Extra Utah wheat would not move the national price. The cost of $276 an acre-foot is the measure of the income a grower gives up by switching; our crop-switching payments are set to cover it.

### Hemp (new research)

*This subsection is new research, added for this document and not part of the verified research base. We could not open the pages it rests on, only search summaries of them, so its figures should be checked before they are relied on.*

Hemp is sometimes proposed as a low-water crop. The evidence we found does not support that. A Purdue estimate puts hemp's season water need at 25 to 30 inches of rain and irrigation combined, and Colorado State University field work found irrigated hemp yielded far more than unirrigated hemp, with the researcher describing its water use as high compared with other crops ([Hemp Industry Daily, n.d.](https://hempindustrydaily.com/?p=89461)). That is close to what alfalfa needs from irrigation alone in the basin, so switching would save little water.

The market is also thin. US hemp production was worth $445M in 2024 from 45,294 planted acres, and floral hemp made up 87% of the value grown in the open; grain hemp was worth $2.62M nationally ([USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service, 2025a](https://data.nass.usda.gov/Newsroom/Executive_Briefings/2025/04-17-2025.pdf)). A basin switching tens of thousands of acres would add a large share of a small national crop and drive its price down. We therefore do not count on hemp, and our crop-switching programme is costed on wheat.
