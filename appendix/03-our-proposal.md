# 1.3 Our proposal

Our proposal is seven instruments that work as one system: price signals that make water scarce, programmes that free water cheaply, rights the state buys and holds, a mandate as a backstop, and monitoring that makes sure the water arrives. Together they put 678 KAF of net water into the lake in their first year, 922 KAF by 2030, 910 KAF in 2035 and 857 KAF in 2055. All figures below are from the model at the settings in tab 1.1.

## 1. Monitoring and verification

**What it does.** Monitoring pays for meters, gauges, water accounting and the legal work of protecting dedicated water as it travels down the rivers. It buys no water itself. Its job is to make sure the water we pay for reaches the lake instead of being taken by a downstream diverter.

**What it delivers.** It lifts the share of dedicated water that arrives at the lake from 0.85 to 0.898, and adds to how much water the river system can shepherd each year. By 2055 the shepherding capacity is 895 KAF a year, against 850 KAF without the extra monitoring.

**What it costs.** $50M a year, half the public budget, and $1,830.6M over 2027–2055. It is the largest single line because every other instrument depends on it.

## 2. Water rights and lease-to-own agreements

**What it does.** The state leases water from willing sellers and holds it as dedicated water for the lake. A lease-to-own agreement lets part of each payment count towards buying the right outright, at a price of $6,000 an acre-foot.

**What it delivers.** The state holds 280 KAF of rights from the first year, which is the most willing sellers will offer: the model's lease and sale capacity is the binding limit, not money. Together with repossessed water, 694 KAF a year is shepherded to the lake by 2055. The lease rate rises from $75 to $135 an acre-foot as holdings grow. At $2M a year, no right is ever owned outright: paying one off would take 43.1 years, past the 2055 horizon.

**What it costs.** $289.1M over 2027–2055 from the budget. Across all shepherded water in 2055, the state pays $30.25 an acre-foot in lease payments.

## 3. The scarcity charge

**What it does.** A charge on each acre-foot depleted by agriculture, mineral extraction, industry, commerce and institutions: 1,938.4 KAF a year in all. Household use and reservoir evaporation are outside it. The rate starts at $85 an acre-foot. A thermostat moves it toward a target each year, and an escalator pushes it higher while the lake stays short of the goal. As the lake recovers, the charge falls away.

**What it delivers.** In 2027 the rate is $87 and users cut 248.5 KAF a year in response. By 2035 the rate has fallen to $9 and the cut to 26.4 KAF; by 2055 the rate is close to zero. The charge does its work early, when the lake is lowest, and then steps aside as bought water takes over.

**What it costs.** It costs the public nothing; it raises $147.3M in 2027 and $17.7M in 2035. Its cost falls on the users who pay it and on the farm output they forgo, which we count in the full cost (tab 1.8).

## 4. The crop levy

**What it does.** A levy of $400 for each acre-foot a hay field applies above 1.5 acre-feet an acre. It targets the heaviest irrigators first. Of the levy revenue, 90% is returned to farmers through the crop-switching programme.

**What it delivers.** 211,371 acres of hay ground fall within its scope. On those acres the average excess over the threshold is 0.91 acre-feet, and growers cut half of it. That frees 148.2 KAF a year of depletion.

**What it costs.** Growers pay $41.6M a year, of which $37.5M comes back to them as crop-switching payments.

## 5. The mandate and repossession

**What it does.** The mandate is a backstop. It sets a floor of 1,200 KAF a year of reduced depletion and orders only what the voluntary instruments have not already found. Where the market falls short, the state can repossess water rights, paying for them in instalments at 3% interest, up to 140 KAF a year. No penalty is set: a farm under the mandate is ordered to cut, not fined.

**What it delivers.** The mandate carries the early years. It orders 599 KAF in 2027 and 571 KAF in 2035, and only 153 KAF by 2055, once bought and shepherded water has grown. Repossession reaches 549.6 KAF by 2035. Ordered water is harder to track than bought water, so the model assumes only 71% as much of it arrives.

**What it costs.** Repossessed rights are paid for from the rights budget. The farm revenue lost on ordered water is not compensated, and is counted in the full cost.

## 6. Conservation programmes

**What they do.** Four programmes free water directly. Cloud seeding adds winter snow. Phragmites removal replaces a thirsty invasive reed in the lake's wetlands with native plants. Crop switching pays farmers to move from alfalfa to lower-water crops such as winter wheat. On-farm efficiency upgrades irrigation systems.

**What they deliver and cost.**

| Programme | Spending ($M a year) | Water to the lake (KAF a year) | Cost per acre-foot delivered |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cloud seeding | 1.5 | 50, at its cap | $28.6 |
| Phragmites removal | 25 | 36.9 | $678 |
| Crop switching | 40.0 (2.5 from the budget, the rest from the levy) | 100, at its cap | $398 |
| On-farm efficiency | 19 | 18 | $1,056 |

Together they deliver 204.9 KAF a year. Cloud seeding and crop switching are at their caps: the limit is how many storms and how many willing farms there are, not money.

## 7. Residential lawns

**What it does.** The model can ask households to cut lawn watering and enforce it with fines. **What it delivers.** Our proposal asks for no cut, so it delivers nothing and costs nothing. Polling shows 58% of Utah voters prefer incentives and 22% prefer penalties ([Deseret News & Hinckley Institute of Politics, 2026](https://www.deseret.com/politics/2026/05/27/poll-utahns-concerned-about-water-great-salt-lake/)), and lawn savings reach the lake poorly (tab 2A.7).

## How the money is spent

![Yearly spending by line, 2027–2055. Every year spends the full $100M.](blob/a92a3cf7-c90f)

Every year spends the whole $100M. Over 2027–2055 that is $2,900M: monitoring $1,830.6M, rights $289.1M, phragmites $553M, on-farm efficiency $115.1M, crop switching $72.1M from the budget and cloud seeding $40M. The yearly schedule front-loads monitoring, so in 2027 the whole $100M goes to it.

## How the instruments work together

The instruments hand the work from one to another over time. In the first years, while the lake is lowest, the scarcity charge and the mandate do most of the work, because they act at once. At the same time the programmes and the levy start freeing water at low cost, and the levy's revenue pays for crop switching. As rights are bought and repossessed, and as monitoring raises shepherding capacity, bought water takes over: by 2055, 69.8% of the water moved is bought, 148 KAF comes through prices and 153 KAF is ordered. The charge then falls almost to zero.

![Water by lever in 2055.](blob/697a311b-ccb4)

The result is a package that does not rely on any one instrument. Without monitoring, bought water would leak to downstream users. Without the charge and mandate, the lake would wait years for the market to grow. Without bought water, the lake would depend on orders that are harder to defend and harder to track.
